An Anthropic mathematician says he and Claude have proved the Riemann Hypothesis, a 167-year-old maths problem with a $1 million prize; Lean experts back the proof

The proof is written entirely in computer-checked code. Experts who build the checking software say they are confident it is correct, but no traditional peer review has happened yet.

The claim

Levent Alpöge, a mathematician who works at Anthropic, posted a note on 20 November saying he believes that, working with the AI model Claude, he has found a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis.

First posed by Bernhard Riemann in 1859, the hypothesis is about how prime numbers are spread out along the number line. It is one of the most famous unsolved problems in mathematics and one of the Clay Mathematics Institute's Millennium Prize Problems, worth $1 million.

A proof checked by a computer

Unusually, there is no human-readable paper. The proof is an archive of 175,187 files in Lean 4, a programming language in which every logical step is checked by software. Alpöge says a written version would be enormous.

On 21 November, the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative — the groups behind Lean and its main maths library — said in a joint statement they have "full confidence" the proof is correct. MIT mathematician Andrew Sutherland compiled it independently, but said there is "much work to be done" to understand the details.

What to keep in mind

This is still a claim. There has been no conventional peer review, and Anthropic had not commented as a company when the reports came out. Alpöge also says the work covers the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, a wider version of the problem.

Even so, if it holds up, it would be a landmark moment both for mathematics and for AI, which until now has mostly helped with smaller steps rather than the field's biggest questions.

💡 Explained

What is the Riemann Hypothesis?

Prime numbers — 2, 3, 5, 7, 11 and so on — look scattered at random, but mathematicians have long suspected a hidden order. In 1859 Bernhard Riemann linked their spacing to a special function, the zeta function, and guessed that all its important zeros sit on one straight line. If true, it tells us how evenly primes are spread out. Nobody had proved it for 167 years, which is why a computer-checked proof is such a big claim.
